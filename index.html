The model

felt share = ln(age ÷ m)ln(lifespan ÷ m)   m = age of earliest memory

In 1877 the philosopher Paul Janet suggested that a year feels as long as the share of your life it makes up. If each moment feels shorter in proportion to how long you've been alive, then adding up those moments gives a logarithm. Your felt time is counted from your first memory, because that is where your remembered life begins.

This is a model, not a measurement. Studies find that people of all ages feel days and weeks passing at about the same speed. Older adults do say that decades seem to go faster, and that is the effect this model describes.

03 · The good news

The curve assumes every year is like the last. It doesn't have to be.

Looking back, we judge how long a stretch of time was by how much we remember from it. A year of routine leaves few new memories and folds up small. A year full of firsts, people and attention unfolds long. Research on memory, attention and wellbeing points to four things that make your time feel longer when you look back on it.

i.

Novelty

New experiences create new memories, and memories are what past time is measured by. This is the "holiday paradox": a new trip flies by while you're on it, then feels long when you remember it. Most people's best-remembered years are from their teens and twenties, when so much happened for the first time.

Try: change a route, learn something with a steep beginning, go somewhere you can't navigate on autopilot.

Hammond, Time Warped (2012) · Avni-Babad & Ritov (2003) · Rubin, Rahhal & Poon (1998)

ii.

Fulfillment

Meaningful work and absorbing challenges put you in flow. Hours pass without you noticing, but they leave behind things you made and moments you remember. Moments of awe also help: in experiments, people who felt awe said they had more time available and felt less rushed.

Try: protect one block a week for the thing that absorbs you; look for awe on purpose, in nature, music or scale.

Csikszentmihalyi, Flow (1990) · Rudd, Vohs & Aaker, Psychological Science (2012)

iii.

Relationships

The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed its participants for more than 85 years. Its clearest finding is that the quality of people's relationships predicts health and happiness in later life better than any other factor it measured. Emotional moments are also remembered more strongly, and the moments that move us most usually involve other people.

Try: fewer "catch-ups", more shared firsts. Do something new with someone.

Waldinger & Schulz, The Good Life (2023) · McGaugh, Annual Review of Neuroscience (2004)

iv.

Presence

In one large study, people's minds were wandering during nearly half of their waking hours, and they were less happy when that happened. You can't remember what you didn't notice. Paying attention is how a moment becomes a memory, and people who have just meditated tend to judge time intervals as longer.

Try: once a day, fully notice one ordinary thing: its light, sound and texture. That's a memory you've kept.

Killingsworth & Gilbert, Science (2010) · Kramer, Weger & Sharma, Consciousness and Cognition (2013)

For you

Method & sources

Weeks are counted as 52.18 per year. The felt share is ln(age/m) ÷ ln(lifespan/m), which follows from assuming that the felt length of each moment is proportional to 1/age (Janet, 1877). The "calendar" line in the chart counts time from your earliest memory, so the two lines can be compared directly. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.
