How it's calculated

If each year feels shorter in proportion to how long you've already lived (Janet, 1877), the share of your remembered life that you've felt is:

ln(age ÷ first memory) ÷ ln(lifespan ÷ first memory)

This is a model, not a measurement. People don't feel days going faster as they age, but they do say decades pass faster. Weeks are counted as 52.18 per year. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

The research behind "make it feel longer"

Novelty
Looking back, we judge time by how many distinct memories it left. Routine shortens time in retrospect, and new experiences lengthen it: a new trip flies by while you're on it and then feels long when you remember it (Avni-Babad & Ritov, 2003; Hammond, 2012). Most people's best-remembered years are from their teens and twenties, when so much happened for the first time (Rubin et al., 1998).
Fulfillment
Absorbing, meaningful work leads to "flow" and leaves memories behind (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). In experiments, moments of awe made people feel they had more time available (Rudd, Vohs & Aaker, 2012).
Relationships
In the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has run for more than 85 years, relationship quality was the strongest predictor of health and happiness later in life (Waldinger & Schulz, 2023). Emotional moments are also remembered more strongly (McGaugh, 2004).
Presence
In one large study, minds wandered during nearly half of waking hours, and people were less happy when they did (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010). People who had just meditated judged time intervals as longer (Kramer, Weger & Sharma, 2013).
