Why life seems to speed up

Each year is a smaller slice

  • Fewer firsts

    Childhood is full of first times. Adult weeks tend to repeat, and routine weeks leave fewer memories, so they shrink when you look back.

    Avni-Babad & Ritov, 2003

  • More rush

    The more rushed people feel, the faster they say time goes. In surveys, this predicts the speed-up better than age does.

    Friedman & Janssen, 2010

The years that feel long are usually the ones full of memories.

What research suggests fills them

  • Something new

    Routine makes time shrink when you look back. New places, skills and experiences leave more memories behind, so the same weeks feel longer.

    Avni-Babad & Ritov, 2003; Hammond, 2012

  • Meaningful work

    Absorbing, meaningful activity is one of the most studied sources of wellbeing. In small experiments, moments of awe made people feel they had more time.

    Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Rudd, Vohs & Aaker, 2012

  • People you love

    In a Harvard study that has run for more than 85 years, close relationships predicted health and happiness later in life better than anything else.

    Waldinger & Schulz, 2023

  • Paying attention

    Our minds wander during nearly half of our waking hours. What you don't notice, you can't remember.

    Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010

How it works

A year feels as long as the share of your life it makes up. At 5, a year is a fifth of everything you've lived. At 50, it's a fiftieth. The philosopher Paul Janet described this in 1877.

Add up a lifetime of shrinking years and you get a logarithm. The count starts at your earliest memory, because that's where the life you can look back on begins:

felt so far = ln(age ÷ first memory) ÷ ln(lifespan ÷ first memory)

Everything is calculated in your browser. Nothing you enter is sent anywhere.

How accurate is this?

Treat the percentage as a thought experiment, not a measurement. Here's what the evidence does and doesn't support.

It's a hypothesis
Janet's idea is almost 150 years old, and no study has measured how long a whole life feels. The number shows what follows if the idea is right.
Surveys only partly support it
People of all ages say days and weeks pass at about the same speed. Older people do say years and decades go faster, but the effect is modest and levels off around middle age. How busy people feel predicts it better than their age does.
The exact curve is unknown
Other versions of the idea bend less. A square-root version (Lemlich, 1975) would put you at 52% instead of 70%.
Memory doesn't follow the curve
The model gives your earliest years the most weight, but most people remember little from before about age 7, and the most from their teens and twenties.
The advice rests on mixed evidence
The link between routine and time shrinking in hindsight is the most direct. The relationship findings come from long observational studies, which show association rather than cause. The awe and meditation results come from small experiments.

The research in more detail

Something new
Looking back, we judge how long a stretch of time was by how many distinct memories it left. Routine makes time shrink in hindsight, and new experiences stretch it. A new trip flies by while you're on it, then feels long when you remember it. Most people remember their teens and twenties best, when so much happened for the first time.
What matters to you
Absorbing, meaningful activity ("flow") is one of the most studied sources of wellbeing. In small experiments, moments of awe made people feel they had more time available.
Other people
The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed its participants for more than 85 years. The quality of their relationships predicted health and happiness later in life better than anything else the study measured. Emotional moments are also remembered more strongly.
Being present
In one large study, people's minds wandered during nearly half of their waking hours, and they were less happy when that happened. People who had just meditated judged time intervals as longer.
